02 / background

Why this project exists.

The B.Tech thesis at VNIT Nagpur (2023–24) and its paper, condensed to what a reviewer will ask about first.

The problem

Blanket spraying wastes chemicals

Weeds compete with crops for light, water and nutrients. The usual fix is to spray the whole field uniformly, which wastes weedicide, costs money and harms the soil and the surrounding area.

Site-Specific Weed Management (SSWM) means spraying only where weeds actually are. That needs a map of where the weeds are and a vehicle that can act on it.

The idea

Drone sees → model maps → drone sprays

  1. Inspect: a UAV photographs the field from above.
  2. Segment: a CNN labels every pixel as weed or not.
  3. Quantify: the field is split into cells and the weed fraction of each cell is measured.
  4. Plan: cells above a threshold become waypoints in a visit order.
  5. Act: a sprayer UAV flies the route (here, in Gazebo simulation).
The research claim

RGB + vegetation indices

Multispectral cameras (NIR, red-edge) separate plants well, but they are expensive. The paper “Enhancing Crop Monitoring and Weed Detection with Vegetation Indices” (Borade, Sharma, Pandey, Surender, VNIT) shows that converting cheap RGB into ExG, ExGR and CIVE before the network gives higher accuracy than raw RGB.

Reported: 92.22% with RGB → 95.04% with indices (+2.82%), using DeepLabV3+.

04 / data preparation

From raw flights to training pairs.

All of this happens in training.py → find_pairs(), read_pair() and split_pairs(). Counts below were measured on the bundled data.

Step 1 · the dataset

2018 WeedMap, RedEdge camera

A public sugar-beet dataset from ETH Zürich (Sa et al., Remote Sensing 2018). A UAV flew pre-programmed paths with a MicaSense RedEdge multispectral camera. The images were stitched into orthomosaics and cut into tiles with pixel-level labels.

Each tile comes in many bands (R, G, B, NIR, RE, CIR, NDVI), but only the RGB band is used. That is the whole point: the method should work with an ordinary camera.

Step 3 · turning colour annotations into targets 3 COLOURS → 2 CLASSES

WeedMap labels are colour images. The project only cares about weeds, so crop and soil are merged into “other”. A pixel is a weed when red > 200 and green, blue < 80. The thresholds tolerate slight compression noise. If a label differs in size from the tile, it is resized with nearest-neighbour so no new in-between colours are invented.

Class imbalance is the central difficulty. In the training split, only 3.56% of pixels are weed (crop is 4.39%; everything else is soil or no-data). A model that predicts “no weed” everywhere would already score about 96% accuracy. This is why the loss is weighted and why weed IoU is reported, not just accuracy.

Step 4 · split by flight, then augment 326 TRAIN · 72 VALIDATION

Why split by flight and not randomly? Neighbouring tiles from the same flight overlap and look almost the same. A random split would put near-duplicates in both sets, so validation would measure memorisation. split_pairs() shuffles the flight IDs with seed 42 and holds out round(5 × 0.2) = 1 whole flight, which is flight 003. If only one flight exists, it falls back to a sorted path split.

Augmentation: each training sample gets an independent 50% chance of a horizontal flip and of a vertical flip, applied identically to image and target. A top-down field has no natural “up”, so flips are realistic variations for free.

Research-stage note

The original notebook used a random 80/20 split of filenames (train_et.txt / test_et.txt), giving about 318 / 80 tiles. That is what the paper reports. The flight-level split here is stricter, so the numbers are not directly comparable. That is worth saying in the review before someone asks.

Reproducibility: random, numpy and torch are all seeded with 42 in main().
05 / segmentation model

What the network sees.

RGB pixels become three vegetation features. A compact U-Net predicts a weed or other class at each pixel.

U-Net, simplified

Four encoder blocks, a bottleneck, and four upsampling blocks. Each decoder block joins a same-resolution encoder skip feature.

Each block uses two 3×3 convolutions with batch normalization and ReLU. Max pooling downsamples; transposed convolutions upsample. Output logits are selected with argmax.

Why a U-Net?

  • Encoder learns what is in the image (texture, leaf shape) at coarse resolution.
  • Decoder recovers where it is, back at full resolution.
  • Skip connections pass fine edge detail straight across, which matters because weeds are only a few pixels wide.
  • Small widths (16→256) keep it at 1.95 M parameters, so it trains on a laptop GPU. DeepLabV3+ with ResNet-50 has roughly 20× more.

Feature channels

Implemented in model.py: RGB uint8 is converted to float32 and scaled to [0, 1].

ExG
Excess greenEmphasizes green relative to red and blue.
ExGR
Excess green minus redCombines green and red excess terms.
CIVE
Color index of vegetation extractionWeighted color combination with an offset.
ExG = 2g − r − b
ExGR = 3g − 2.4r − b
CIVE = 0.441r − 0.811g + 0.385b + 18.78745/255

Why indices instead of raw RGB?

  • Plants reflect much more green than red or blue, and soil does not. Each index turns that into a single number where vegetation stands out.
  • Brightness changes from sun or shadow mostly cancel in the differences, which helps with different flights and times of day.
  • The network starts from features that already separate plant from soil, so it can spend its capacity on weed vs crop shape and texture.
  • Formula note: the paper writes ExGR = ExG − (1.4R − G). Expanding it gives exactly 3G − 2.4R − B, as used in code. The CIVE offset 18.78745 is on the 0–255 scale, hence the /255.

The same vegetation_features() function runs in training and inference, so the model always sees identically prepared inputs.

How the model learns.

LossCrossEntropyLoss(weight=…)Per-pixel classification loss. The class weights make a missed weed pixel cost more than a missed background pixel.

How the metrics are computed evaluate() · 2×2 CONFUSION MATRIX

Every validation pixel lands in one cell. torch.bincount(target × 2 + prediction) fills all four cells in one pass.

predicted weed
predicted other
true weed
TPweed found
FNweed missed
true other
FPfalse alarm
TNcorrectly ignored
  • Accuracy = (TP + TN) / all. Misleading here because TN dominates.
  • Precision = TP / (TP + FP): of the pixels flagged as weed, how many really were? Low precision means wasted spray.
  • Recall = TP / (TP + FN): of all real weeds, how many were found? Low recall means weeds survive.
  • Weed IoU = TP / (TP + FP + FN): overlap of the predicted and true weed regions. It ignores TN entirely, so it is the honest single number for this task.
For spraying, recall matters more than precision. A missed weed patch keeps spreading, while a slightly over-sprayed area only costs a little extra chemical.

How to present the comparison honestly
  • The paper's model is much larger (DeepLabV3+), trained on 448×448 resized tiles with a random split.
  • From the paper's own matrix, weed IoU ≈ 2.70 / (2.70 + 0.03 + 11.00) ≈ 0.20: very high recall but many false alarms.
  • This pipeline reaches 0.51 weed IoU on an unseen flight, a harder test, with about 20× fewer parameters, by trading a little recall for much better precision.
  • Different splits and models mean this shows direction, not a controlled benchmark.

From a mask to a flight path.

planner.py turns pixels into metres. Try it below with the real mask predicted for tile 003 / frame0034.

Cells are shaded by weed fraction. Cells below the threshold are skipped. The route starts at the origin (0, 0), bottom-left, and always flies to the closest remaining cell. This is the same greedy rule as treatment_waypoints().




The maths in planner.py
  1. Weed fraction per cell = number of mask pixels equal to 0 ÷ pixels in the cell. The grid is cut with integer division, so uneven sizes still cover every pixel.
  2. Threshold: keep the cell if fraction ≥ --min-weed-fraction (default 0.05 = 5%).
  3. Pixel → metre: cell width = field width ÷ cols.
    x = origin_x + (c + 0.5) · cell_w
    y = origin_y + (rows − r − 0.5) · cell_h
    The flip makes image row 0, the top, become the far +y edge, as in Gazebo/ROS where y points up.
  4. Order: greedy nearest-neighbour from the start point using math.hypot.


Assumptions to state in the review
  • The image is orthographic and top-down and shows the whole rectangular field. There is no GPS or georeferencing in this code; field size and origin are given by hand.
  • Nearest-neighbour is a fast heuristic, typically within about 25% of the optimal tour. It is not guaranteed shortest.
  • The output is a CSV for manual use. The pipeline never arms or commands a drone.
  • The weed fraction is written per waypoint, so a sprayer could scale its dose by it (variable-rate spraying). That step is research-stage only.




Research stage: prescription maps prescription_maps/ · pmap_gen.ipynb




A prescription map tells a variable-rate sprayer how much chemical each zone gets. In the notebooks the field mask was split into patches, and the weed % of each patch was scaled to 0–255 to form a grayscale dose map. Each patch centre became a node carrying a dose value, and the map was blended with the weed map for display (0.7 × prescription + 0.3 × weed map).
A general weighted formula was drafted, with only the weed and NDVI terms switched on (w2 = 50, w3 = 10):
P = w1·yield + w2·weed% + w3·NDVI − w4·fert_limit − w5·weedicide_limit − w6·gov_standard





Research stage: three path algorithms compared path_planning.ipynb




Why this pipeline uses nearest-neighbour: it sits between them. Like the priority and TSP approaches it visits only weedy cells, but it runs in O(n²), so it scales to large grids where exact TSP becomes intractable.


Simulation hand-off FYP · Drone/waypoints.py




The research repo includes a ROS + MAVROS node for a PX4 autopilot, tested only in Gazebo SITL (software-in-the-loop). It:
  1. waits for the flight-controller connection, then streams 100 setpoints (PX4 requires a setpoint stream faster than 2 Hz before OFFBOARD mode);
  2. switches to OFFBOARD mode and arms;
  3. publishes the current waypoint on mavros/setpoint_position/local at 20 Hz;
  4. moves on once x, y and z are all within 0.1 m and the per-waypoint dwell time has passed (the spraying window).
The CSV x/y/z columns from this pipeline can be pasted into its waypoints list. That is the “manual Gazebo handoff”.

13 / review prep
Questions you will be asked.
Short, defensible answers grounded in the code. Click to expand.




Data


Why only RGB when WeedMap has NIR and NDVI?
Multispectral cameras are expensive and rare on farm drones; RGB cameras are on every drone. The research question was whether RGB-derived indices can recover some of the benefit. NDVI needs a NIR band, so it cannot be computed from RGB, but ExG, ExGR and CIVE can.


Why drop 572 of the 970 tiles?
Those tiles sit on the edge of the orthomosaic, where 40% or more of the pixels are black no-data. They teach the model nothing about weeds and would push accuracy up artificially. The 40% cut-off came from the research notebook and gives the 398 tiles reported in the paper.


Why merge crop and soil into one class?
The action is binary: spray or don't. A crop pixel and a soil pixel both mean “don't spray”, so a 2-class problem matches the decision and is easier to learn. The cost: the model is never told explicitly that sugar beet is not a weed, and it has to learn that from shape and texture alone.


Why split by flight instead of randomly?
Adjacent frames overlap and look nearly identical. A random split leaks them into both train and validation and overstates performance. Holding out a whole flight (003) tests on genuinely new imagery, which is how the system would be used on a new field.


Model & training


Accuracy is 98% but IoU is 0.51: which one is right?
Both are correct; they measure different things. About 96% of pixels are not weed, so accuracy is dominated by easy background. IoU ignores true negatives and only asks how well the predicted weed region overlaps the real one. That is why the checkpoint is chosen by weed IoU.


Why a small U-Net instead of DeepLabV3+ from the paper?
For a standalone, reproducible pipeline: it trains from scratch in minutes on a modest GPU, needs no pretrained download, and its skip connections preserve the fine detail of small weeds. DeepLabV3+ is stronger on large, varied datasets, but with 326 training tiles a smaller model is less prone to overfitting.


What does the weighted loss actually change?
Without weights, predicting “other” everywhere gives a low loss, so the model learns to ignore weeds. With weights of 3.75 for weed and 0.72 for other, a missed weed pixel costs about 5× more. The square-root dampening stops it swinging to the opposite extreme of flagging all green as weed.


Why are predicted weed patches “fatter” than the ground truth?
Two reasons. The weighted loss deliberately favours recall, and the decoder upsamples from coarse features, which blurs boundaries. Precision is 0.58 while recall is 0.81. For spraying this is acceptable, since a spray nozzle covers an area anyway.


Planning & system


How do pixels become real-world coordinates?
Linearly: the image is assumed to cover exactly field-width × field-height metres, top-down. Each cell centre is scaled into metres and the y axis is flipped so image row 0 is the far edge. Real deployment would need georeferencing: GPS tags or ground control points to map pixels to coordinates, as described in the thesis.


Why nearest-neighbour and not TSP?
Exact TSP (tried in the notebooks with dynamic programming) grows exponentially and becomes infeasible beyond about 20 cells. Nearest-neighbour is O(n²), deterministic, easy to explain, and usually close to optimal on grid layouts.


Does this fly a real drone?
No. It writes a CSV of waypoints. The separate waypoints.py from the research repo was flown only in PX4 + Gazebo simulation. Keeping the CSV as a manual handoff is a deliberate safety boundary.


Limitations and future work?
Limitations: one crop (sugar beet) and one camera; crop and weed share a class boundary learned only from RGB; no georeferencing; greedy routing; no dose calculation in this pipeline; validation is a single held-out flight. Next steps: cross-validate across all five flights, add a Dice term to the loss (as in the paper), morphological clean-up of the mask, GPS-based georeferencing, per-cell dose from weed fraction, and closing the loop by feeding the CSV directly to MAVROS.


Heads-up: a number in the paper that may draw a question
The paper reports 95.04% accuracy, but the diagonal of its own normalised confusion matrix sums to 2.70% + 86.27% ≈ 89%. The notebook's printed matrix also gives about 89.8%. The likely cause is different evaluation runs, or excluding masked pixels in one but not the other. If asked, acknowledge it and point to IoU on a held-out flight as the more reliable metric.
